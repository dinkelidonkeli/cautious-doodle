AI Summit 2026, Logomo, Turku, 16 November 2026

I was in the room. Here’s what it made me think.

Most posts after an event stop at “great to be there”. I want to try something else: what was said on stage, and what it actually made me think.

AI Summit 2026 in Turku was organised by ELLIS Institute Finland, AI Finland and Foundation PS. I followed the research, enterprise and economy panels. The enterprise and economy panels stayed with me the most.

ELLIS Panel, 10:55–11:35

Research: the next leap needs rebels

Moderated by Eric Malmi, with Danica Kragic, Matthias Bethge, Kuan Yen Tan and Erkki Oja.

On stage

  • Before the panel, Samuel Kaski’s ELLIS keynote: the institute has recruited researchers from around the world (I noted 19), and health is its first big research focus.
  • So far, models have been trained on human-made data. We are now entering a time when AI-generated data has to carry more of the load.
  • Robots do backflips, but they break a lot and are expensive. Materials still need real innovation, and human senses remain far superior.
  • Europe is not ahead. Quantum computing could add speed.
  • Erkki Oja, the inventor of Oja’s rule, was a rebel in the 1980s who did not believe in rule-based expert systems. With Teuvo Kohonen, he was one of Finland’s early neural network pioneers.
  • LLMs are the dominant trend with no strong challenger, and we no longer really know how they work. They run on text, which is a rigid code. A four-year-old knows things without reading a word.
  • Backpropagation (Hinton et al., 1986) took 40 years to go from idea to working systems. We need out-of-the-box thinking alongside LLMs.
  • Jobs are going to change and we need to adapt very quickly. Oja’s view: the intelligence of LLMs is overstated.

My take

I don’t have the scientific background to dispute Oja, or even to analyse it properly. But the largest investment in human history is being built on these models. It can’t all be hot air.

What I keep coming back to is language. Natural human language is hardly the most efficient way to steer AI. It feels resource-hungry. I expect models to work in some other “language” in future, the way human-readable code is compiled into something leaner before a computer runs it.

And Oja’s call for rebels, or punks as I’d put it, is surely right. LLMs shouldn’t be the only option in the AI era. History shows that the most popular theories aren’t necessarily the winners.

As for Europe: it isn’t ahead, even though significant investments are still being made. The EU’s AI Gigafactories aim to mobilise €20 billion, but construction of the first one is only planned to start in 2027. Is it still too little, too late?

ELLIS Panel, 11:35–12:15

Enterprises: stop piloting the edges, redesign the core

On stage

  • Pekka Lundmark: small companies have an adoption gap, large companies a scaling gap.
  • Value comes when you redesign the work. That happens more in start-ups, which is why this is not a question for the IT department.
  • Lundmark: redesign your core processes using AI, not 30 pilots. It has to be on the CEO’s and the board’s agenda, with a dedicated team, freedom to act and the CEO’s backing.
  • Stage 1 was creating and distributing models. Stage 2, real use of AI, is only beginning.
  • The data at the heart of industry is the next important training material, as the internet’s data has already been used. The bottleneck is building transmission grids; new energy can be brought online quickly.
  • Running this transformation the traditional way, through middle management, may be too slow. That layer may also be concerned about its own future.

Finnish companies using AI as a deliberate choice to improve the business11%

Using a tool is not the same as redesigning the work. The 38% is from Statistics Finland, Use of information technology in enterprises 2025. The 11% was cited on the panel.

Also on stage

  • In Europe, IT is still seen as a back-office function, and AI too often lands there. The business should be the driver.
  • Changing processes has been hard partly because systems have chained us: change is hard and expensive. That constraint may no longer exist.
  • A company’s edge is coded into its ERP, but there is no manual for how it got there. Company knowledge hides in Oracle and SAP silos.
  • Accenture’s view: a new AI layer will sit on top of the systems of record.
  • Real success comes from co-creation, not from procuring process automation.

My take

It’s clear that using data straight from the cores and tables of core systems is very hard. Embedded AI, built into the core systems themselves, will bring some help.

But the most important thing is to understand your current processes, and many companies struggle with that. Their processes were last documented perhaps during the previous ERP renewal, and even then the documentation never told the whole truth.

Will AI sit on top of the ERP, or change it?

I’ve written about the possible scenarios at more length. Here is the short version of how I see the paths.

A new layer on top

AI sits above SAP and Oracle, and the ERP becomes a back office of interfaces and business logic.

My view: that alone doesn’t justify what these systems cost, and changing them stays expensive and complex.

AI inside the ERP

Embedded AI arrives in the core systems themselves.

My view: it helps, but it can’t fix processes that nobody fully understands. This transformation will also be slow, and will it ever really match your processes?

Rebuilt beside the ERP

The process that gives you an edge is rebuilt next to the ERP, one process or module at a time.

My view: this is the one I’m watching. It starts from the company and from AI, not from the system.

Why have companies shaped themselves around the processes their systems offer, when it should be the other way round?

The question this panel left me with

Panel 1, 13:15–14:30

Economy: why wait for the public sector?

On stage

  • New technology lowers the cost of processes.
  • Björn Wahlroos: the biggest change will come in the public sector. A large number of people handle simple tasks there, and there is no price component. With AI, the welfare state could become affordable.
  • On what AI should do for people:

    Does it make you feel better, or does it make you better? Heroin makes you feel better.

  • Jussi Westergren: the tools keep changing. If you think Claude is the last thing we’ll see, you’re wrong. Europe needs more ambition.
  • Trust in the leaders of the leading AI companies is low, and that is why people are scared.
  • Big tech, not carmakers, leads in autonomous driving. How did that happen?
  • Westergren and Peter Sarlin: Europe has huge potential, but the fight for the brightest minds is on, and many have already gone to the US.
  • Edward Jung: Europe is the one asking what the human role is in all of this. He sees that as one of the biggest differences from the US and Asia.
  • The most entertaining panel of the day, and it didn’t feel overly scripted.

My take

Why not think about this in the business world first? Tasks where people process data by rules are just as interesting for companies, for automating processes and making them more efficient.

Edward Jung’s point is a good one, but it calls for careful balance. Europe must not regulate itself into an AI backwater. Otherwise the brain drain, and the drain of investment, will keep flowing to where the money is and the frame is freer.

What I take home

The same pattern keeps repeating. The biggest gains come from rethinking a company’s core processes.

That is also the hardest part. Pekka Lundmark, who has led large organisations, said it plainly: changing processes is very difficult. And I agree with Accenture that the rigidity and cost of systems has held it back too.

So are we entering a time when this starts to change, one process or module at a time? When the process that gives you a competitive edge is rebuilt beside the ERP, starting from the company and from AI?

A thread running through the day

Where is the productivity leap?

On stage

  • Speakers kept coming back to the same point: the productivity leap from AI does not show in the statistics yet.

My take

But did anyone see one a year after the steam engine was invented? Or even a year after the first truly safe and working steam engine was widely available?

Comparing the physical and the digital world is a bit unfair, I admit. Still, 2026 and 2027 will probably tell whether the effect really starts.

In software development it is already clear and part of everyday work. There the question is rather how we organise ourselves anew, and what the business models of development will be when it is no longer time multiplied by an hourly rate.

Meanwhile, AI runs on a different clock

Steam needed six decades before it even matched water power. Between last year’s AI Summit and this one, twelve months were enough to shake whole markets.

  1. November 2025Three frontier models in twelve daysOpenAI’s GPT-5.1 (12 Nov), Google’s Gemini 3 (18 Nov) and Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.5 (24 Nov). Opus 4.5 passed 80% on SWE-bench Verified, a test of fixing real software issues.
  2. January 2026Agents move into office workAnthropic introduced Claude Cowork on 12 January, and on 30 January added plugins for legal, finance, sales and marketing work.
  3. February 2026The “SaaSpocalypse”Software stocks fell hard. Thomson Reuters dropped 16%, and a software stock index lost about a quarter of its value between 12 January and 23 February. Investors began questioning per-seat licences and billable hours.

Cybersecurity had to rethink how flaws are found.
  6. April 2026Another wave in one weekClaude Opus 4.7 (16 Apr), OpenAI’s GPT-5.5 (23 Apr), pitched for real work and agents, and DeepSeek’s open-weight V4 preview (24 Apr).

Would I recommend it, and go again?

Absolutely.

You will hardly see a line-up of speakers this strong at any other Finnish event, on a topic this current. It almost annoys me that I have to wait a year for the next one.

Maybe a new model for events could be smaller gatherings, more often, because AI moves like a bullet train.

Then again, many panelists referred back to what they had said a year ago. Some of the fundamentals and challenges have stayed much the same.
